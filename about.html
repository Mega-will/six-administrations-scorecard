Six Administrations Scorecard · Overview

How to read this

A sourced, two-sided record of what happened under each U.S. president from Clinton to Trump's second term, 1993 to 2026. It reports what each administration did, the case its defenders make, the case its critics make, and who else deserves credit or blame. It does not rank anyone, and it was built so that you can check any number yourself.

What this is

There are two parts, and they answer different questions.

The chart page answers "what happened to ordinary life?" It tracks thirteen measures of the typical household, including pay, work, prices, poverty, health coverage, housing, lifespan, overdoses, crime and the federal debt, from 1992 to 2026. Every chart has the party that held the White House shaded behind it and the party that held the House and the Senate drawn underneath. A matrix shows the change over each term, and a roll-up averages the yearly changes by party. The page shows who presided over each change. It cannot say who caused it, and the roll-up makes that plain: Democratic-president years come out ahead on 9 of 13 measures, but drop the four years dominated by 9/11, the financial crisis and COVID-19 and it is 7 to 6. Group the same years by control of Congress instead, and divided-government years lead.

The scorecard answers "what did each administration actually do?" It covers 100 policy domains in ten groups, and each domain has one row for each of the six administrations. Each row is the same four things: the facts, the defenders' case, the critics' case, and who else holds credit or blame.

How to read a cell

Every domain has six rows, one per administration, and every row has the same four fields.

  • Facts. What happened, with dates, figures and vote counts. Each figure links to the page it came from.
  • Defenders say. The strongest case that administration's supporters make, in their framing.
  • Critics say. The strongest case its critics make, in theirs.
  • Attribution. Who else was involved: which Congress passed or blocked the law, what the Federal Reserve and the courts did, and which events nobody chose. This field is there because an outcome in a president's first year usually belongs to the predecessor, and most of these years were divided government.

The labels

  • A figure with no label was traced to a page that was opened and read, and it links to that page. Click through before you argue from it.
  • approx. means the figure was not confirmed on an opened primary page. It may be right, but treat it as unverified.
  • IN PROGRESS marks Trump's second term, which is 21 months old as of this writing. Those rows are a snapshot, not a record.
  • Dates on moving figures. Casualties, vote counts, court status and economic releases carry an as-of date. Anything dated 2026 will move.
  • "Partisan page not opened" or "not opened" in a Defenders or Critics field means the line states that side's argument as it is commonly made, but it could not be tied to a specific page from that camp.
  • Legal words are exact. Indicted, dismissed, pleaded guilty, convicted, acquitted and pending are used as the cited source uses them, with the date.

What it will not do

No cell says which president or party was better. The two "say" fields are each side's best case, written with the same effort for every administration. If you find a cell that reads as a verdict, that is a defect, and the fix is a source, not a rewording.

How it was checked

The scorecard was researched one bucket at a time in late October 2026, then reviewed twice. Each review is listed with what it found, so that you can judge how much to trust a given kind of claim.

October 28, 2026: independent fact-check

A checker who had not seen the drafting tested 61 claims. 44 were confirmed, 10 were corrected and 7 could not be verified either way. All 20 of the sampled figures from government data series matched their source. Every 2026 event that was tested checked out on its date and its core fact.

November 11, 2026: second review

  • Chart data. About 230 year-points on the chart page were re-checked against their source pages and 14 values were changed, most of them FBI crime revisions. On the corrected series, violent crime fell slightly under Trump's first term; the first version had shown a rise. The 9-to-4 and 7-to-6 tallies did not change.
  • Gap closing, all ten buckets. About 550 weak spots were worked. Roughly 310 figures were confirmed with a link to a primary page, about 150 were corrected or updated, and about 30 claims the cited sources did not support were removed. More than 100 congressional vote tallies now link to the House Clerk or Senate roll-call page. Figures labeled approx. fell from about 480 to 269.
  • Corrections that changed meaning, not just a digit: the foreign-policy bucket had said Congress rejected Iran war-powers limits in 2026, when the House passed three resolutions and the Senate agreed to one; an unsupported reason for the attorney general's firing was removed; January 6 figures now say "charged", not "convicted"; a Supreme Court decision described as exempting only the Fed in fact leaves the Fed's status undecided; Trump 47 payroll growth had been understated; two trade claims the sources did not support were removed; net-interest figures from 2015 on had been drawn from the wrong series and were re-sourced.
  • Consistency. Eleven facts that appear in more than one bucket now read the same everywhere, each with a date.

November 12, 2026: this site

These pages are generated from the reviewed text by a script that compares every cell back to its source after building. Every one of the 3,029 links in the scorecard cells and every character of cell text is checked to be unchanged on each build. The counts on this page are produced by that script, not typed.

What is still weak

In the order that matters most to a reader.

  1. Foreign Policy (bucket 4) is the least finished. It carries 79 figures labeled approx. and 30 notes of an unopened source, mostly in the Clinton and Obama rows and the critics' fields of domains 4.8 to 4.10.
  2. Legal status in Rule of Law (bucket 8) moves. Not confirmed past October 2026: Bolton's sentencing; any appeals ruling on the dismissals of the Comey and Letitia James cases; whether Section 702 surveillance authority has been reauthorized.
  3. The "say" fields are mostly summaries. Outside the Biden and Trump 47 rows, most Defenders and Critics lines summarize a known position without linking a named outlet. Only about 56 citations go to a partisan outlet's own page, 30 right-leaning and 26 left-leaning.
  4. Some numbers rest on Wikipedia alone. Dollar totals for the large spending laws, disaster death tolls and damages, bank-failure counts, CHIPS award amounts, estimates for the big trade deals, judge and incarceration totals, press-freedom ranks, and several Trump 47 figures.
  5. 2026 snapshots that will move.
    • Midterm: Senate D 54, R 44 with Mississippi and Alaska uncalled; House D 254 on AP calls with 16 uncalled, as of Nov 10. A Louisiana runoff is Dec 12.
    • Iran war: 23 U.S. deaths as of Nov 1; no ceasefire in force.
    • Tariff refunds: $126B of $166B as of Oct 2. No ruling found in the litigation over the replacement tariffs.
  6. Secondary sources where an agency page would not load. The FBI's 2025 crime release (news reports in bucket 9), several DHS figures in bucket 5, the highway-bill status in bucket 7, and the lead-pipe court status.
  7. Sampling limits. The independent fact-check tested 61 claims of roughly 2,400 cells. Assume a few dozen undetected errors of the same kind remain. Any single number you intend to argue from: click through and confirm.

On the chart page

  • Violent crime uses the FBI's older rape definition through 2014 and the revised one from 2015, which adds about 10 per 100,000. Obama's cell crosses that splice and understates the fall. Crime for 2000 to 2014 could not be re-opened at the FBI on Nov 11 and stands on the earlier read.
  • The 2020 jump in weekly earnings reflects lower-paid workers losing jobs; 2020 and 2021 disposable income includes relief payments; the Census says 2019 and 2020 income and poverty were flattered by survey nonresponse. These affect the end of Trump 45 and the start of Biden.
  • 2025 overdoses and 2025 crime are provisional or first-release figures, and every 2026 value is a partial year. No October 2025 household survey or price index was collected, so 2025 averages cover 11 months.
  • Some uninsured-rate years were not individually re-checked, and the 2024 poverty rate may have been revised by 0.1 point since it was read.
  • Inflation is computed from the seasonally adjusted index, life expectancy is the World Bank series, and debt is the calendar fourth-quarter value. Each can differ from the headline figure by about 0.1.

Limits no amount of checking fixes

  • The roll-up describes coincidence. Four shock years move the partisan tally from 9 to 4 to 7 to 6.
  • Outcomes lag policy by one to ten years. A first-year statistic mostly belongs to the predecessor.
  • 20 of the 34 years were divided government. Congress, the Federal Reserve, the courts and the states drove much of every cell.
