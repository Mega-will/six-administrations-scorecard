Six Administrations Scorecard · Bucket 6 of 10

Trade, Industry & Technology

Across six administrations that alternated between free-trade agreements (Clinton, Bush 43, Obama) and tariffs (Trump 45, Biden, Trump 47), the goods-and-services trade deficit widened from $70 billion (1993) to a $924 billion peak (2022) and manufacturing employment fell from 16.8 million to 12.68 million in October 2026, with neither strategy yet reversing either trend.

6.1

Trade agreements (NAFTA/USMCA, WTO accession, KORUS, TPP, Phase One)

Measures the binding trade pacts each administration negotiated or ratified; they set the rules under which a state's exporters, importers and workers compete.

6.2

Tariffs and trade enforcement (Section 232/301, IEEPA tariffs)

Measures use of tariffs and trade-remedy law; tariffs are a state's most direct lever on imports, prices and foreign leverage.

AdministrationFactsDefenders sayCritics sayAttribution
DClinton (1993–2001)No broad tariff actions; the Uruguay Round cut tariffs and moved disputes to WTO panels after 1995; the average effective U.S. tariff stayed in the low single digits for the whole period (Yale notes 9.1% in 2026 was the "highest since 1946 excluding 2025") (Yale Budget Lab); Section 301 was used as a negotiating threat (e.g., Japan autos, 1995, approx.).Low tariffs and binding WTO rules delivered cheap imports and the low-inflation expansion of the 1990s while holding trading partners to enforceable commitments.Labor-aligned critics say the near-zero-tariff regime with Mexico and then China accelerated factory job displacement (EPI's 682,900 NAFTA estimate; 2.0–2.4 million China-shock losses) (China shock).The 104th–106th Congresses (R-R) favored liberalization; WTO creation passed under the Democratic 103rd. Import surges from China came after 2001, outside the term.
RBush 43 (2001–2009)Section 201 steel safeguard tariffs of 8–30% took effect March 20, 2002, exempting Canada and Mexico; the WTO ruled against them Nov 11, 2003 and authorized over $2 billion in retaliation; lifted Dec 4, 2003; USITC's central estimate was a $41.6 million welfare loss; a CITAC-funded study claimed about 200,000 steel-consuming jobs lost, while the ITC noted steel-consuming employment rose 53,000 March–Dec 2002 (Wikipedia).The tariffs were temporary, WTO-sanctioned breathing room for a consolidating steel industry; production rose slightly and the measures were withdrawn once the industry restructured.Lake & Liu (2025) found the tariffs "did not boost local steel employment" and depressed steel-consuming employment for years; the WTO loss and EU retaliation threats forced retreat; Read (2005) found costs exceeded benefits.Executive action under Section 201 (107th Congress, R House/D Senate); the reversal was driven by the WTO ruling and threatened EU retaliation, not Congress.
DObama (2009–2017)No Section 232/301 tariffs; a Section 421 safeguard on Chinese tires (Proclamation 8414, Sept 11, 2009: 35%, then 30% and 25%, over three years from Sept 26, 2009) (Federal Register) and 2012 antidumping duties of 18.32–249.96% plus countervailing duties of 14.78–15.97% on Chinese solar cells (Commerce archive) were the main actions; brought WTO cases against China; the effective tariff rate stayed in the low single digits (Yale Budget Lab).Targeted, WTO-consistent enforcement protected specific industries without a trade war, and the administration pursued China through WTO litigation rather than unilateral tariffs.PIIE estimated the tire tariff cost consumers about $1.1 billion in 2011 to save at most 1,200 jobs, at least $900,000 per job (PIIE); Republicans and some Democrats said he was too passive on Chinese currency manipulation and overcapacity.Executive action; the 112th–114th Congresses (R House) did not legislate tariffs. The post-2008 collapse in imports, not policy, drove the 2009 deficit drop.
RTrump 45 (2017–2021)Section 201 tariffs on solar (30%) and washers (Jan 23, 2018); Section 232 steel 25% and aluminum 10% (March 2018); Section 301 on China in tranches of $34 billion (July 6, 2018), $16 billion, $200 billion and $300 billion (Sept 2019); by Jan 2020 tariffs covered 16.8% of imports (Wikipedia); Fed economists found tariff-exposed industries had relative employment declines (Fed); CBO estimated −0.5% GDP and −$1,277 per household in 2020 (CBO).Tariffs brought China to the Phase One table, revived idle steel capacity, forced KORUS and USMCA renegotiations, and were retained by Biden, which supporters read as vindication.A QJE study found $51 billion in losses to U.S. consumers and importers with near-full pass-through; PIIE put the cost above $900,000 per steel job saved; retaliation required a $28 billion farm bailout (Wikipedia).Executive authority under 201/232/301 with a Republican 115th Congress declining to intervene; retaliation and the 2020 COVID collapse confound outcome data.
DBiden (2021–2025)Kept the Section 301 China tariffs and 232 metal tariffs; extended solar 201 tariffs four years (2022) and washers two years (2021) (Wikipedia); the May 14, 2024 four-year review raised China rates, finalized Sept 13, 2024 and effective Sept 27, 2024: EVs 100%, solar cells 50%, EV batteries, critical minerals, steel and aluminum 25% (Wikipedia)."Targeted and strategic" tariffs defended IRA-funded industries against Chinese overcapacity without the broad consumer tariffs of his predecessor, and allies were brought along via tariff-rate quotas.Free-trade economists note he kept tariffs he had called self-defeating; 100% EV tariffs slow EV adoption that his climate policy sought; no tariff relief was offered during the 2021–23 inflation.Executive; the 117th (D-D) and 118th (R House) Congresses did not act. The inflation spike was COVID and 2022 energy-shock driven, not tariff-driven.
RTrump 47 (2025– ) IN PROGRESSIEEPA tariffs from Feb 1, 2025; April 2 "Liberation Day" 10% baseline plus country rates; Section 232 tariffs on steel and aluminum (50%) and autos (Wikipedia); Supreme Court, 6–3, Feb 20, 2026: "IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs" (Justia); a 10% Section 122 tariff (Feb 24–July 24, 2026) (Wiley) gave way July 24, 2026 to Section 301 tariffs of 10%/12.5% on 60 economies covering 86 countries (Yale); a challenge by states and importers was argued at the CIT Sept 30, 2026 and was undecided at last report (Bloomberg/Transport Topics), and the May 7, 2026 CIT ruling against the Section 122 tariff is stayed on appeal (Troutman); IEEPA tariff refunds: $166 billion owed, $126 billion refunded as of Oct 2, 2026 (FactCheck).IEEPA tariffs raised an estimated $142 billion in 2025 without a price spike, produced 15% deals and investment pledges, and CBO scored continued tariffs at $2.4–3.0 trillion of deficit reduction over a decade (Brookings/Riedl).The Court found no authority; the effective rate hit 16.9%, highest since the 1930s; the New York Fed found nearly 90% of costs fell on U.S. firms and consumers, about $1,100 per household (Brookings, Yale).Entirely executive; the Republican 119th Congress passed no tariff law and did not extend Section 122; courts (CIT, Federal Circuit, Supreme Court) drove the reversal. The Section 301 tariffs stay in force pending the CIT's decision; their effects are not yet measurable.

6.3

Goods and services trade deficit

Measures the gap between what the country sells and buys abroad (Census/BEA FT-900), a marker of competitiveness and dependence on foreign capital.

AdministrationFactsDefenders sayCritics sayAttribution
DClinton (1993–2001)Goods-and-services deficit widened from $70.3 billion (1993) to $369.7 billion (2000); the goods deficit went from $132.5 billion to $446.8 billion while the services surplus rose from $62.1 billion to $77.1 billion (Census FT-900 history); the goods deficit with China was $83.8 billion in 2000 (Census).A widening deficit reflected the strongest growth in the G7, a strong dollar and foreign capital flooding into U.S. investment, not weakness; services exports grew throughout.The five-fold widening coincided with NAFTA and the run-up to PNTR; left and populist critics argue it foreshadowed the manufacturing losses of the 2000s.Deficits are driven by savings-investment balances, dollar strength and the Asian crisis (1997–98) more than trade policy; Congress R-R from 1995.
RBush 43 (2001–2009)Deficit went from $360.4 billion (2001) to a then-record $763.5 billion (2006), 5.5% of GDP approx., and $712.4 billion in 2008; the goods deficit hit $837.3 billion in 2006 (Census); the China goods deficit rose from $83.1 billion (2001) to $268.0 billion (2008) (Census).Cheap imports raised real incomes and the deficit financed investment; the record 2006 gap was partly an oil-price effect; it shrank sharply as oil fell and the recession hit.The tripling of the China deficit tracked the "China shock" (2.0–2.4 million manufacturing jobs lost) and a 25%-plus cumulative housing-era import binge; critics on both sides say Treasury never confronted China's currency peg.Oil prices, the housing bubble and China's 2001 WTO entry (Clinton-negotiated) dominated; 2008 collapse was crisis-driven; Congress R-R to 2007, D-D after.
DObama (2009–2017)Deficit fell to $394.8 billion in 2009 as imports collapsed, then widened to $479.5 billion in 2016; the services surplus more than doubled from $114.9 billion to $270.3 billion while the goods deficit reached $749.8 billion (Census); the China goods deficit was $346.8 billion in 2016 (Census).The deficit stayed well below the 2006 peak as a share of GDP; services and energy exports surged (shale), and the goods deficit ex-China was stable.The China goods deficit grew another 29% from 2008 to 2016; a 2015 TPA fight and TPP showed the administration prioritized new deals over enforcement, critics on the left and right argued.The 2009 narrowing was the Great Recession, not policy; the shale boom (private, state-regulated) cut petroleum imports; Congress R House from 2011.
RTrump 45 (2017–2021)Deficit rose from $516.9 billion (2017) to $559.3 billion (2019) and $646.0 billion (2020); the goods deficit reached $912.9 billion in 2020 (Census); the China goods deficit peaked at $418.2 billion (2018) then fell to $342.6 billion (2019) and $308.0 billion (2020) under Section 301 tariffs (Census).Tariffs cut the bilateral China goods deficit by 26% from 2018 to 2020 and shifted sourcing to Mexico and Vietnam; the overall deficit rise reflected a strong economy and dollar.The overall deficit, which Trump pledged to eliminate, grew 25% over the term; the China gap moved to other Asian suppliers rather than disappearing; the 2020 goods deficit set a record.Trade diversion, not reshoring, explains the China decline; 2020 figures are COVID-distorted; Congress R-R then D House.
DBiden (2021–2025)Deficit hit $837.3 billion (2021) and a record $923.7 billion (2022), eased to $774.2 billion (2023) and was $903.5 billion in 2024; the goods deficit reached $1,215.4 billion and the services surplus a record $311.9 billion in 2024 (Census); the China goods deficit fell to $279.6 billion (2023) and $297.0 billion (2024) (Census).The China goods deficit fell to its lowest since 2010 (Census) as tariffs and CHIPS/IRA reshoring took hold; record services exports and a demand-led import boom reflected the fastest G7 recovery.The overall deficit set records in 2022 and neared them in 2024; IRA/CHIPS spending pulled in imported equipment; critics on the right say "Bidenomics" subsidized imports.Post-COVID stimulus demand and a strong dollar drove imports; China's decline partly reflects transshipment via Mexico and Vietnam; Congress D-D then R House.
RTrump 47 (2025– ) IN PROGRESS2025 deficit was $901.5 billion with a record goods deficit of $1,240.9 billion (tariff front-running) and record services surplus of $339.5 billion (Census); January–September 2026 deficit was $662.3 billion, down $92.4 billion (12.2%) from $754.6 billion a year earlier, with exports up 11.6% and imports up 6.2% (Census FT-900, Sept 2026); the China goods deficit fell to $202.7 billion in 2025 and $131.5 billion for January–September 2026 (Census).A 12% year-to-date narrowing in 2026 and, in 2025, the lowest China goods deficit since 2005 show tariffs working as intended, supporters argue, with exports growing faster than imports.Brookings' Gale and Patel note deficits "have not noticeably narrowed" relative to 2024 levels; 2025 front-loading inflated the base, and the 2026 comparison partly reflects that distortion (Brookings); the year-to-date narrowing shrank from 19.9% through August to 12.2% through September, when the monthly China goods deficit reached $21.9 billion, the year's highest (Census).Executive tariffs; the 2025 record goods deficit was import front-running ahead of tariffs; the Feb 2026 Supreme Court ruling and Section 122/301 switch mean 2026 data mix regimes. Full-year 2026 data not yet available.

6.4

Manufacturing employment and reshoring

Measures factory jobs (BLS CES) and new plant investment; manufacturing capacity underpins defense, supply-chain security and middle-income work.

AdministrationFactsDefenders sayCritics sayAttribution
DClinton (1993–2001)Manufacturing employment rose from 16.79 million (Jan 1993) to a peak of 17.64 million (March–April 1998), then eased to 17.11 million (Jan 2001), a net gain of about 315,000 (FRED/BLS); the sector's share of total jobs fell as services grew faster.The only one of the six administrations under which manufacturing employment ended higher than it began; the 1998 peak came four years after NAFTA.Labor economists note the decline began in 1998 with the Asian crisis and strong dollar, and that PNTR in 2000 set up the 2001–2010 collapse.Congress R-R from 1995; the 1998 Asian financial crisis and strong dollar drove the late-term decline; China effects lagged into the next term.
RBush 43 (2001–2009)Manufacturing employment fell from 17.11 million (Jan 2001) to 12.55 million (Jan 2009), a loss of 4.55 million jobs, the largest of any administration (FRED/BLS); studies attribute 2.0–2.4 million of the 2000s losses to Chinese import competition, others only 550,000 (China shock).Productivity, not trade, explains most losses: output kept rising with fewer workers; consumers gained ("$411,000 per lost manufacturing job" in lower prices per Jaravel and Sager).Autor, Dorn and Hanson found exposed regions suffered lasting unemployment and wage losses with "offsetting employment gains" that "have yet to materialize"; manufacturing then lost about 1.2 million more jobs between Dec 2007 and Jan 2009 (FRED/BLS).China's 2001 WTO entry (Clinton-negotiated) and the 2001 and 2008 recessions dominate; Congress R-R to 2007; the final-year drop was crisis-driven.
DObama (2009–2017)Employment bottomed at 11.44 million (Feb–March 2010) and recovered to 12.33 million (Jan 2017), up 890,000 from the trough but 220,000 below the Jan 2009 level (FRED/BLS); the auto rescue cost Treasury a net $9.3 billion on about $80 billion invested (Treasury).The first sustained manufacturing job growth since the 1990s followed the auto rescue and Manufacturing USA institutes; the sector added jobs in each calendar year from 2010 through 2015.Jobs never regained the 2008 level; growth stalled in 2015–16 as the dollar rose and oil investment fell; critics say TPP would have deepened offshoring.The 2010 trough reflects the inherited recession; recovery was cyclical and Fed-supported; the 2015–16 stall tracked the dollar and oil bust. Congress R House from 2011.
RTrump 45 (2017–2021)Employment rose from 12.33 million (Jan 2017) to 12.76 million (Dec 2019, +426,000), then fell to 12.14 million (Jan 2021) after COVID, a net loss of 192,000 (FRED/BLS); Fed economists (Flaaen and Pierce, 2019) found industries more exposed to the 2018 tariffs had relative employment declines (Fed).Pre-pandemic gains of 426,000 in under three years show, supporters say, tariffs plus tax cuts beginning to reverse a decade of offshoring.The Fed study found tariffs lowered manufacturing employment on net via input costs and retaliation; growth slowed in 2019 before COVID; the term ended with fewer factory jobs than it began.Pre-2020 gains continued the 2010–2019 cyclical trend; the 2020 loss was COVID-driven; Congress R-R then D House.
DBiden (2021–2025)Employment rose from 12.14 million (Jan 2021) to 12.90 million (Dec 2022) and ended at 12.67 million (Jan 2025), a net gain of 531,000 (FRED/BLS); construction spending on manufacturing plants tripled from $76.4 billion (Jan 2021) to a $250.2 billion annual rate (Sept 2024) (FRED/Census); groups tracked 751 announced clean-energy and chip projects worth $422 billion by Jan 2025 (Wikipedia/IRA).CHIPS and IRA triggered a factory-construction boom unmatched since the 1960s and the highest manufacturing employment since 2008; the jobs lag the plants.Employment flattened then fell after Dec 2022 despite the spending; critics on the right say subsidies bought construction, not sustained jobs, and on the left that few plants were unionized.Early gains were COVID rebound; construction surged after the 2022 laws (117th Congress, D-D 50-50; CHIPS had 17 Senate Republicans); hiring at new plants lags by 2–4 years.
RTrump 47 (2025– ) IN PROGRESSEmployment fell from 12.67 million (Jan 2025) to 12.58 million (Dec 2025), then rose to 12.68 million (Oct 2026), a net gain of about 8,000 so far (FRED/BLS, FRED); manufacturing construction spending fell from a $237.5 billion annual rate (Jan 2025) to $170.2 billion (Sept 2026) (FRED/Census); Japan's $550 billion pledge targets chips, pharma, shipbuilding and energy (White House).Tariffs plus allied investment pledges ($550 billion Japan, $600 billion EU claimed) are rebuilding core industries; the 2026 employment uptick shows the turn beginning after an adjustment year.Brookings notes manufacturing employment "stayed roughly flat"; input tariffs on steel and aluminum raise costs for the 80-to-1 majority of manufacturers who use rather than make metal; plant construction is down 32% from peak.Executive tariffs; the IRA-credit rollback in the July 2025 reconciliation law (119th Congress, R-R) cut announced clean-energy plants; Fed rates and the 2025–26 tariff uncertainty weigh on investment. Hiring from pledged foreign investment is not yet measurable.

6.5

Industrial policy (CHIPS, IRA, Buy American, strategic sectors)

Measures direct state support for chosen industries, which decides whether critical goods (chips, batteries, steel, vaccines) can be made at home.

AdministrationFactsDefenders sayCritics sayAttribution
DClinton (1993–2001)Expanded the Advanced Technology Program (begun 1991) and dual-use technology efforts; from 1995 the Republican Congress repeatedly recommended ATP's termination (Wikipedia); the Partnership for a New Generation of Vehicles (1993) and the Internet's commercialization were the era's flagship public-private programs.Clinton-Gore "technology policy" seeded the commercial Internet and biotech boom while keeping government to pre-competitive research, a light-touch model that paid off in the late-1990s productivity surge.Conservatives (Cato, Heritage) labeled ATP "corporate welfare" that funded large firms' research; the Republican Congress cut it yearly; PNGV produced no mass-market vehicle before Bush ended it.The 104th–106th Congresses (R-R) constrained spending programs; the 1990s tech boom was private-capital driven and the Fed accommodated it.
RBush 43 (2001–2009)American Competitiveness Initiative (Jan 31, 2006): $50 billion over ten years for research and $86 billion in R&D tax incentives, $5.9 billion committed in FY2007 (Wikipedia); the America COMPETES Act (Aug 9, 2007) repealed ATP; the Energy Policy Act of 2005 created DOE loan guarantees (approx.); Treasury began auto loans in Dec 2008 under TARP, part of about $80 billion eventually invested (Treasury).Bush favored tax incentives and basic research over picking winners, and the late-2008 auto bridge loans prevented a disorderly GM-Chrysler collapse during the crisis.Critics on the right say the auto loans violated free-market principles; on the left, that ACI pledges were never fully appropriated and manufacturing lost 4.5 million jobs with no response.ACI needed the Republican 109th Congress and then the Democratic 110th; the auto loans were a crisis response in the final weeks; the 2008 shock drove the pivot.
DObama (2009–2017)Auto rescue: Treasury invested about $80 billion, recovered $39.7 billion from GM and $19.6 billion from Ally, exited GM Dec 9, 2013 and Ally Dec 18, 2014, at a net cost of $9.3 billion (Treasury); Solyndra's $535 million DOE loan guarantee (2009) ended in bankruptcy Sept 1, 2011 with a $528 million loss; a four-year DOJ/IG probe closed in 2015 without charges (Wikipedia); ARRA added clean-energy manufacturing credits (amounts approx.).The auto rescue saved an estimated million-plus jobs approx. for $9.3 billion net, and the DOE loan portfolio overall returned interest exceeding its losses (Tesla repaid early).Solyndra became the emblem of political "picking winners"; House Republicans found restructuring put private investors ahead of taxpayers; Americans for Prosperity spent $8.4 million attacking it.The 111th Congress (D-D) passed ARRA; the auto program was inherited from Bush's Dec 2008 loans; the 2008 crisis forced the interventions.
RTrump 45 (2017–2021)"Buy American and Hire American" order (EO 13788, April 18, 2017) (Federal Register) and Section 232 tariffs used as industrial policy; Foxconn's 2017 Wisconsin deal promised 13,000 jobs for nearly $3 billion in state credits and was cut in 2021 to 1,454 jobs and $80 million (WPR); Operation Warp Speed backed six vaccine candidates, two authorized in Dec 2020 (GAO-21-319); the Defense Production Act was invoked for ventilators and vaccines in 2020; Phase One purchase pledges were 57% met (GTR/PIIE).Tariffs and deregulation revived steel and aluminum investment, and Operation Warp Speed delivered vaccines in under a year, the most successful industrial mobilization since World War II in supporters' view.Foxconn employed 1,029 people at end-2022 against 13,000 promised (WPR); tariffs raised input costs for downstream manufacturers; critics say there was no coherent strategy beyond tariffs until COVID forced one.Executive orders and tariffs with a Republican 115th Congress; Warp Speed drew on CARES Act funds passed by a Democratic House; COVID-19 drove the 2020 interventions.
DBiden (2021–2025)CHIPS and Science Act (Aug 9, 2022): $52.7 billion for semiconductors including $39 billion in incentives and a 25% investment credit; awards to Intel ($7.86 billion), TSMC ($6.6 billion), Samsung ($6.4 billion), Micron ($6.1 billion) (Wikipedia); IRA (Aug 16, 2022; Senate 50–50 with the Vice President's tie-break): energy and climate provisions were scored at about $391 billion (approx.), later estimates up to $1.2 trillion (Wikipedia), and CBO put the law's net deficit reduction at $58.1 billion over 2022–2031 (CBO); factory construction spending tripled to $250 billion/yr by Sept 2024 (FRED).TSMC raised its Arizona commitment to $100 billion-plus, 751 projects worth $422 billion were announced, and the U.S. regained leading-edge chip fabrication, supporters say, reversing 30 years of offshoring.Costs ballooned to two to three times CBO's score; CHIPS research agencies were underfunded by over $8 billion against authorization; critics on the right call it subsidies for firms that would have built anyway.117th Congress: CHIPS passed with 17 Senate Republicans, IRA on a party-line 50–50 with the Vice President's tie-break; plants take years, so job outcomes lag into 2026–2028; Fed rate hikes raised financing costs.
RTrump 47 (2025– ) IN PROGRESSThe July 4, 2025 reconciliation law ended the EV credit after Sept 30, 2025 and limited wind/solar credits to projects under construction by June 2026 (Wikipedia); the government took a 9.9% stake in Intel (Aug 22, 2025) for $8.9 billion, drawn from $5.7 billion of unpaid CHIPS grants and $3.2 billion of Secure Enclave funds (Intel), raised the chip credit by 10 points and denied Natcast $7.4 billion (Wikipedia); May 21, 2026 quantum awards of $2 billion, half to IBM, took minority equity in nine firms (Fiscal Times).Taxpayers now get equity upside (Intel, quantum firms) instead of grants, tariffs give domestic producers durable protection, and allied capital is being steered into chips, shipbuilding and pharmaceuticals.Free-market conservatives object to state ownership stakes; clean-energy groups say repealing IRA credits stranded announced plants; manufacturing construction has fallen 32% from peak; Lutnick's Natcast closure was called political.The 119th Congress (R-R) passed the IRA rollback 50–50 in the Senate with the Vice President's tie-break and 218–214 in the House; the Intel stake used Biden-era CHIPS money; trade-deal investment pledges are non-binding. Outcome data not yet available.

6.6

Federal R&D and technology leadership

Export controls and intellectual-property protection

Measures controls on sensitive technology exports and defense of patents and copyrights; both guard the technological edge a state depends on.

Agriculture and farm policy (farm bills, subsidies, trade retaliation payments)

Measures farm-income support and the fallout of trade fights on growers; food production and rural economies are a strategic base for any state.

AdministrationFactsDefenders sayCritics sayAttribution
DClinton (1993–2001)The 1996 farm act ("Freedom to Farm," April 4, 1996; House 318–89, Senate 74–26) (CRS R45210) ended acreage controls and government grain purchases, shifting to fixed payments tied to historic acreage and requiring crop-insurance enrollment (Wikipedia); when prices collapsed in 1998–2001 Congress added ad hoc "market loss" payments; PNTR (Oct 10, 2000) opened the Chinese soybean market (China shock).Decoupled payments freed farmers to plant for markets, and PNTR and NAFTA made China and Mexico top export destinations for U.S. grain and meat.Conservatives note "Freedom to Farm" was supposed to phase out subsidies but ended in record bailouts; farm groups say the 1996 act left no safety net when prices fell.The 1996 act was written by the Republican 104th Congress; the 1998 Asian crisis crashed commodity prices; ad hoc payments came from a Republican Congress and Clinton together.
