National Security & Defense

Defense spending swung from 5.7% of GDP (1993) to 3.8% (2000), back up to 5.5% (2010) and down to 3.6% (2025) (BEA via FRED), while the use of force migrated from the congressionally authorized wars of 2001–2002 to presidential action alone — Kosovo, Libya, Syria, Yemen, the 2025 Iran strikes, Caribbean boat strikes, the Venezuela raid and the 2026 Iran war — with no new war-specific authorization enacted after October 2002.

3.1

Defense budget and force readiness

Measures how much the state spends on its military and whether that force is trained, equipped and ready to fight when called.

DClinton (1993–2001)Defense share of GDP fell from 5.7% (1993) to 3.8% (2000) (BEA/FRED); the 1993 Bottom-Up Review cut the active-force target from 1.6M to ~1.4M and trimmed $60B from the FY1993–97 plan; procurement was frozen near $45B/yr FY1994–98 against a $60B target, roughly half the mid-1980s peak ("procurement holiday"); the Army deployed to contingencies ~15× as often as in the prior decade (CRS R42334).A peace dividend was earned without hollowing the force: the 1994 Defense Science Board found readiness "acceptable in most measurable areas," and even McCain's 1993 review found no 1970s-style collapse (CRS).Heritage (Jan 1994): Aspin conceded the force was underfunded by $31–50B; procurement fell 17% FY93→FY94; Marine equipment readiness dropped below 90% for the first time in a decade (Heritage).Drawdown began under Bush 41's Base Force after the Soviet collapse; D Congress 1993–95, then R Congress 1995–2001 added funds above requests. Iraq/Afghan drawdowns cut spending; readiness effects were still being argued in 2017.

Ukraine aid ran through separate supplementals.

Nuclear posture and arms control

Measures how the state limits, modernizes and deters with the weapons that can end it, and whether treaties constrain its rivals.

DClinton (1993–2001)START I entered into force Dec 5, 1994; the Senate approved START II 87–4 on Jan 26, 1996, but it never took effect (Arms Control Association); the U.S. was first to sign the CTBT (Sept 24, 1996); the Senate rejected it 48–51 on Oct 13, 1999 (Senate) after no committee hearings and 18 hours of debate, the first security treaty defeated since Versailles (Wikipedia); the Agreed Framework (Oct 21, 1994) froze North Korea's plutonium reactors and reprocessing (ACA).The testing moratorium held, Soviet-successor warheads were secured under Nunn-Lugar, and the CTBT loss was a party-line Senate vote, not a policy failure (Wikipedia).Bush and McCain opposed the CTBT as unverifiable while backing the moratorium (Wikipedia); the U.S. said in Oct 2002 that Pyongyang had acknowledged a covert uranium-enrichment program despite the Agreed Framework (ACA).R Senate 1995–2001 controlled ratification. RTrump 47 (2025– ) IN PROGRESSGolden Dome EO Jan 27, 2025; announced May 20 at $175B, raised to $185B (Mar 2026) (Wikipedia); CBO put space-based interceptors alone at $161–542B over 20 years (CBO); $24.4B in reconciliation plus $13.4B in FY2026 appropriations (Air & Space Forces); Iran sites struck June 22, 2025 (Wikipedia); Iran declared the JCPOA ended Oct 18, 2025 (Wikipedia); New START expired Feb 5, 2026 with no replacement and Putin's offer to observe limits to 2027 unanswered (Wikipedia); Sentinel IOC slipped to early 2030s (Wikipedia).Force, not paper, set Iran back ~2 years (Al-Monitor); any new treaty must include China (Wikipedia); Golden Dome answers hypersonic and space threats.First time since 1972 with no U.S.–Russia limits; IAEA warns Iran could reach 90% within weeks (Wikipedia); MIT physicists say Golden Dome could need hundreds of thousands of satellites; Russia and China jointly oppose it (Wikipedia).R trifecta funded Golden Dome via reconciliation; treaty lapse required no vote.
