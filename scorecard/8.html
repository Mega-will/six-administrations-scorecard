    8.1

    Who staffs the Article III bench and how the Senate confirms them; the courts decide the legality of everything else in this scorecard.

    8.2

    Executive power and emergency authorities

    How far presidents act without Congress through orders, emergencies, war powers and statutory stretch; the core question of who governs between elections.

    AdministrationFactsDefenders sayCritics sayAttribution
    DClinton (1993–2001)364 executive orders (Federal Register); 1999 Kosovo air campaign continued past the War Powers Resolution's 60-day mark, with the administration arguing a funding bill implied authorization; the D.C.     RBush 43 (2001–2009)291 executive orders (Federal Register); the Sept. 2001 AUMF and a post-9/11 national emergency still renewed annually (Newsweek); signing statements challenged more than 1,100 statutory provisions, versus roughly 600 by all prior presidents combined (PBS Frontline); warrantless surveillance and military commissions were partly reined in by Hamdan (2006) and Boumediene (2008).Bush officials (Berenson, Goldsmith on Frontline) say signing statements are traditional and bipartisan, and wartime powers under Article II and the AUMF were necessary after an attack on the homeland.The ABA task force (2006) called the signing statements "contrary to the rule of law"; Boston Globe reporting and Hamdan/Boumediene show courts rejecting the unitary-executive claims.Republican Congress 2003–07 rarely checked the executive; 9/11 was the shock that drove the expansion.     RTrump 47 (2025– ) IN PROGRESS291 executive orders as of Nov. 11, 2026 (225 in 2025, 66 in 2026), matching Bush's eight-year total (Federal Register); nine new national emergencies by Oct. 2025 covering tariffs, the border, energy and ICC sanctions (Newsweek); the Court held 6–3 (Feb. 20, 2026) that IEEPA does not authorize tariffs (Supreme Court), and the Court of International Trade ordered refunds on entries carrying roughly $166 billion in duties and deposits, per a CBP filing (Fox Rothschild); IEEPA tariff refunds: $166 billion owed, $126 billion refunded as of Oct 2, 2026 (FactCheck); Iran war-powers votes, 2026: the Senate agreed 50–47 to discharge S.J.Res. 185 (May 19) but rejected the motion to proceed to it 47–50 (June 24); the House agreed to H.Con.Res. 86 215–208 (June 3) and the Senate agreed to it 50–48 (June 23); the House agreed to H.Con.Res. 89 214–208 (July 23), the same day the Senate rejected discharging S.J.Res. 180 47–49, and the Senate rejected H.Con.Res. 89 49–50 (Sept 24); the House agreed to H.Con.Res. 93 220–204 (Sept 15); the Senate's other nine discharge motions on Iran removal resolutions failed (Mar 4–July 30) (Senate roll calls; House Clerk 199, 282, 307; CRS); no authorization was enacted and operations continued (Jewish Insider; Stars and Stripes).The administration says emergencies and Article II let a president act at the speed crises require, and the Court has largely agreed on the emergency docket (27 of 37 rulings as of Nov. 9, 2026, per the Brennan tracker).Critics across parties note the Court rejected the tariff emergency, lower courts found contempt and Vacancies Act violations, and the War Powers resolution's legal effect is unsettled.Republican Congress (119th) has not terminated any emergency; the tariff loss and refunds were court-driven. Outcome data on most 2026 actions not yet available.

    8.4

    Surveillance and privacy (FISA, Patriot Act, Section 702)

    The legal limits on government collection of Americans' communications; the balance between counterterrorism and the Fourth Amendment.

    AdministrationFactsDefenders sayCritics sayAttribution
    DClinton (1993–2001)Pre-dates the Patriot Act framework; nearest precursors were the 1994 CALEA digital-wiretap mandate, the 1993–96 "Clipper chip" key-escrow proposal (abandoned) and roving-wiretap authority sought after Oklahoma City (dates from general record; no primary page opened); the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court operated under the 1978 FISA "wall" between intelligence and criminal investigators.Supporters say the administration modernized wiretap law for digital networks while losing the crypto-wars fight to civil libertarians, leaving strong encryption legal.Civil-liberties groups opposed CALEA and Clipper as built-in backdoors; after 9/11 critics said the FISA "wall" the Clinton DOJ formalized in 1995 hampered counterterrorism.Republican Congress passed the 1996 antiterrorism law; the surveillance architecture later used was built by Congress after 9/11, not in this period.
    RBush 43 (2001–2009)Patriot Act signed Oct. 26, 2001 after 357–66 and 98–1 votes (House Clerk; Senate); reauthorized March 2006 (Wikipedia); the NSA's warrantless Terrorist Surveillance Program (2001–07) was disclosed in Dec. 2005; the 2008 FISA Amendments Act created Section 702 and gave telecoms immunity (PBS Frontline); more than 300,000 National Security Letters were issued over the following decade, each with a gag order (EFF via PBS).Bush officials say the programs prevented follow-on attacks, were briefed to the Gang of Eight, and were ratified when Congress wrote them into statute in 2007–08.Critics (ACLU, EFF, Sen. Feingold) say warrantless wiretapping violated FISA for years and that Congress legalized it retroactively rather than punish it.Republican Congress 2001–07 passed and renewed the Patriot Act; the Democratic 110th Congress passed the 2008 FAA with then-Sen. Obama's vote. 9/11 was the driver.
    DObama (2009–2017)Signed a four-year extension of expiring Patriot Act provisions in 2011; Snowden's June 2013 disclosures revealed bulk phone-metadata collection under Section 215; Section 215 lapsed June 1, 2015 and the USA Freedom Act (June 2, 2015) ended bulk collection, leaving records with carriers (Wikipedia; PBS); Section 702 reauthorized in 2012 and the FBI opened Crossfire Hurricane July 31, 2016 using FISA on Carter Page (Wikipedia).Defenders say Obama created the PCLOB review, ended bulk collection by statute, added FISC amici and released more surveillance data than any predecessor.Civil libertarians say he defended the programs until exposed and prosecuted leakers; conservatives say the Page FISA applications, later found to contain 17 errors, abused the system.Republican House after 2011; the USA Freedom Act passed a Republican Congress after a Second Circuit ruling. Snowden, not policy, forced the change.
    RTrump 45 (2017–2021)Signed the Jan. 2018 six-year Section 702 renewal; Section 215 expired March 2020 when the House passed no extension (Wikipedia); the DOJ IG found 17 significant errors in the Carter Page FISA applications (Dec. 2019) and an FBI lawyer pleaded guilty to altering an email (Aug. 2020) (Wikipedia); the FBI ran up to 3.4 million U.S.-person queries of 702 data in 2021 and 278,000+ improper queries in 2020–early 2022 (Wikipedia).Supporters say Trump exposed FISA abuse against his own campaign and forced the FBI's first real accountability (new FISC procedures, the Clinesmith plea).Critics note Trump nonetheless signed a clean 702 renewal, and that the worst query abuses (Jan. 6 and protest-related searches) peaked on his watch.Republican Congress 2017–19 passed the 2018 renewal; FISC and the DOJ IG, not the White House, drove the reforms.
    DBiden (2021–2025)FISC found "persistent and widespread violations," including queries on 133 George Floyd protest arrestees and more than 20,000 Jan. 6-linked individuals (Wikipedia); the Reforming Intelligence and Securing America Act (signed April 20, 2024; 273–147, 60–34) (House Clerk; Senate) renewed 702 for two years with supervisor approval of U.S.-person queries but no warrant requirement; FBI U.S.-person queries fell from 119,383 (Dec. 2021–Nov. 2022) to 57,094 (2023) and 5,518 (2024) (ODNI transparency report); PCLOB's 2023 report recommended FISC pre-approval of queries (3–2).The administration says internal reforms cut improper queries by more than 90% and that RISAA codified them without blinding the intelligence community.A left–right coalition (Wyden, Lee, EFF, Brennan Center) says RISAA expanded the "electronic communication service provider" definition and left the backdoor-search loophole open.Republican House, Democratic Senate (118th) passed RISAA; the FISC's disclosures, not the White House, forced the compliance overhaul.
    RTrump 47 (2025– ) IN PROGRESSThe administration sought a "clean" reauthorization. Section 702 timeline, 2026: the House rejected an amended five-year extension with warrant language 200–220 (Apr 17) (Daily Signal); a 10-day extension was signed Apr 18 (Wiley; Juris Review); the House passed a three-year bill 235–191 (Apr 29) that the Senate did not take up, and a 45-day extension passed Apr 30 (House 261–111; Senate by unanimous consent) (ABC News); the House rejected a three-week clean extension 198–218 (June 11), with 19 Republicans opposed (Roll Call); the statute lapsed June 12, 2026 (EFF); collection continues under FISC certifications approved Mar 17, 2026 and running to Mar 17, 2027 (PPSA) (Brennan Center); the administration missed a Sept. 16, 2026 deadline to declassify a March 2026 FISC opinion on continuing FBI violations; no reauthorization had been reported when the Brennan page was last updated (Oct. 27, 2026).Grassley and Cotton say letting 702 lapse is "a gamble we can't afford" and that a clean 18-month renewal was blocked by an unlikely coalition.Civil-liberties groups and Senate Democrats say the lapse is leverage for a warrant requirement and closing the data-broker loophole, and that withholding the FISC opinion violates the law.Republican Congress (119th) failed to pass the administration's bill; the lapse is a congressional stalemate. Outcome data on collection after March 2027 not yet available.

    Second Amendment and firearms policy

    Federal gun laws, their enforcement and the constitutional boundary set by the courts; a core liberty for some citizens and a safety question for all.
